01 · Foundations

What is a projectile?

A projectile is an object that is given an initial velocity and then moves through the air under the influence of gravity alone, when air resistance is neglected. The motion of such an object is called projectile motion.

Definition

Projectile motion

It is the curved, two-dimensional motion of an object launched into the air. Near Earth's surface, its ideal path is a parabola.

Examples

Everyday examples

  • A ball thrown at an angle
  • A basketball shot toward a hoop
  • Water flowing from a fountain
  • A long-jump athlete in flight
Important: A projectile does not need to be moving upward. After reaching its highest point, it is still a projectile while it falls.
02 · The two motions

One journey, two independent directions

Horizontal motion

In the ideal model, no horizontal force acts, so horizontal acceleration is zero and horizontal velocity stays constant.

aₓ = 0   and   vₓ = u cos θ

Vertical motion

Gravity acts downward, so vertical acceleration is constant and equal to −g if upward is positive.

aᵧ = −g   and   vᵧ = u sin θ − gt

The horizontal and vertical motions happen at the same time. The horizontal position depends on time, and the vertical position also depends on that same time.

03 · Formula toolkit

Important projectile equations

For a launch speed u at angle θ above horizontal, from level ground, with no air resistance:

Resolve initial velocity

uₓ = u cos θ
uᵧ = u sin θ

These are the horizontal and vertical components of the starting velocity.

Position after time t

x = (u cos θ)t
y = (u sin θ)t − ½gt²

Use these to find where the projectile is at a particular time.

Velocity after time t

vₓ = u cos θ
vᵧ = u sin θ − gt

Horizontal velocity is constant; vertical velocity changes steadily.

At the highest point

vᵧ = 0
t↑ = (u sin θ)/g

Only the vertical component is zero. Horizontal velocity is still present.

Total time of flight

T = (2u sin θ)/g

This applies when the projectile lands at the same height from which it was launched.

Maximum height and range

H = (u² sin² θ)/(2g)
R = (u² sin 2θ)/g

Maximum height is measured above the launch level; range is horizontal distance.

Use g ≈ 9.8 m/s² near Earth's surface (sometimes school problems use 10 m/s²). Keep units consistent.

05 · Worked example

Solve it step by step

Question: A ball is projected from level ground at 20 m/s at an angle of 30° to the horizontal. Find its time of flight, maximum height and horizontal range. Take g = 10 m/s².

1Write the given values

u = 20 m/s, θ = 30°, g = 10 m/s²

2Find the time of flight
T = 2u sin θ / g = (2 × 20 × 0.5) / 10 = 2 s
3Find the maximum height
H = u² sin² θ / 2g = (400 × 0.25) / 20 = 5 m
4Find the horizontal range
R = u² sin 2θ / g = 400 × sin 60° / 10 ≈ 34.6 m
Final answers: Time of flight = 2 s; maximum height = 5 m; horizontal range ≈ 34.6 m.
06 · Key ideas

Facts worth remembering

Parabolic path

The ideal trajectory is a parabola because horizontal position changes uniformly with time while vertical position changes quadratically with time.

Complementary angles

For the same launch speed and level landing, angles θ and (90° − θ) give the same range in the ideal model.

Maximum range

On level ground, the range is greatest at 45° for a fixed launch speed, if air resistance is ignored.

Mass does not appear

In the ideal model, objects launched with the same initial velocity have the same trajectory regardless of mass.

Limits of the model: Real objects experience air resistance and may be affected by wind, spin, and changes in air density. The simple equations are an approximation.
